# Fast Editorial — Calibration Examples

**Status:** GREENFIELD CALIBRATION PACK  
**Purpose:** teach editorial standards through concrete decisions from the successful v3 experiment  
**Canonical:** not yet

## How to use this file

These examples are not templates to imitate mechanically.

They show the kind of editorial judgment Fast Editorial needs to preserve.

Each example follows:

```
initial judgment
→ source finding
→ correction
→ lesson
```

A future calibration pack should also include one or two complete approved historical Master Briefs once their canonical texts are available in the repository.

## 1. Toyota versus Volkswagen

### Initial judgment

Toyota appeared to have overtaken Volkswagen in Europe.

### Source finding

Closer reading showed that the comparison applied to:
- July;
- European private customers.

For January–July, Volkswagen still remained ahead of Toyota by 376 cars.

### Correction

The claim was narrowed to the specific month and sales channel.

It was not presented as a general year-to-date change in European market leadership.

### Lesson

> **Precision of scope is part of meaning.**

Always check:
- period;
- geography;
- segment;
- denominator / comparison base.

A dramatic headline can become false when a limited comparison is generalized.

## 2. Defence-tech

### Initial judgment

The common story could be framed primarily as the rise of private capital in defence technology.

### Source finding

The source mechanisms were materially different.

Russia:
- state mobilization;
- cheap commercial Chinese components;
- rapid iteration.

Western model:
- venture capital;
- startups;
- private investment.

### Correction

The frame was widened from private capital to a broader story about:
- cheaper weapons;
- commercial technologies;
- rapid iteration;
- private capital as one mechanism, not the universal mechanism.

### Lesson

> **A cross-paper frame must fit all included mechanisms.**

Do not force sources into a shared explanation merely because one explanation is elegant.

## 3. Reaction of AI stocks

### Initial judgment

Sources appeared to disagree about whether “AI stocks” rose or fell.

### Source finding

They were describing different market segments.

Selected hyperscalers rose by 2% or more.

The PHLX Semiconductor Index fell by 5.9%.

### Correction

The story became one of market segmentation rather than source contradiction.

### Lesson

> **Different segment is not automatically disagreement.**

Before constructing source tension, check:
- segment;
- metric;
- time;
- geography;
- scope.

## 4. Treasury in the watchlist

### Initial judgment

The underlying rate story had been Deep Read correctly.

### Source finding

The 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.012% intraday but closed at 4.96%.

During writing, a watchlist formulation asked whether the yield would “remain above 5%”.

That wording implicitly treated an intraday crossing as the established closing state.

### Correction

The watch item was changed to ask about a **return above 5% and ability to remain there**.

### Lesson

> **Correct source understanding can still become an incorrect implication during compression.**

This is why Selective Deep Read and Verification are separate controls.

## 5. Editor-in-Chief approval without change

### Initial expectation

An eighth review pass might seem to require an additional edit.

### Source finding

On the documented days, Editor-in-Chief could complete the review and find no justified reason to change the text.

### Correction

No edit was forced.

The edition passed.

### Lesson

> **A review pass must perform judgment, not manufacture change.**

“Pass with no change” is a valid result.

---

## 6. What these examples teach together

The recurring editorial disciplines are:

- do not generalize beyond source scope;
- do not force a common mechanism;
- do not manufacture disagreement;
- verify the concrete final wording, not only the upstream interpretation;
- do not edit good work merely because a review role exists.

These are not isolated factual corrections.

They are examples of how Business Brief protects:
- trust;
- source individuality;
- conceptual accuracy;
- reader understanding;
- editorial restraint.
