# Fast Editorial — Business Brief Spirit, Editorial Purpose & Working Principles

**Status:** GREENFIELD PRODUCT / EDITORIAL INPUT  
**Source basis:** suspended Fast Editorial briefings, Product, Editorial Philosophy, Scope, Lessons and current Project purpose  
**Canonical:** not yet  
**Role in reconstruction:** this document captures the **why**, **how to think**, and **what kind of editorial work Business Brief is trying to perform**. It is deliberately separate from the reconstructed v3 mechanism in `01_WORKFLOW.md`.

---

# 1. Why Business Brief exists

Business Brief does not exist to create another news digest.

Its purpose is more ambitious:

> **Bring the value of excellent foreign journalism to Czech professional readers. Not only the news, but the way strong newspapers and authors think.**

The reader can often learn the headline elsewhere.

Business Brief must justify its existence by bringing what is harder to obtain:

- the author's question;
- the publication's frame;
- the structure of the argument;
- the tension inside the story;
- the uncomfortable counterpoint;
- the unexpected interpretation;
- the mechanism behind the event;
- a perspective that is uncommon in Czech debate;
- an intellectual move that changes how the reader understands the subject.

The product should not merely answer:

> What happened?

It must also ask:

> **What does this author or publication see in what happened?**

---

# 2. Core motto

> **Není důležité, na co se díváš, ale co vidíš.**

Many serious newspapers look at the same election, company, war, rate decision, energy shock or technology shift.

Their value may lie in seeing different:

- causes;
- risks;
- winners and losers;
- mechanisms;
- historical parallels;
- moral tensions;
- strategic implications;
- questions worth asking.

The object of attention may be the same.

The way of seeing it may be radically different.

That difference is editorial value.

---

# 3. The product is not only information compression

Business Brief does not merely compress three newspapers.

It transfers:

- information;
- framing;
- argument;
- doubt;
- perspective;
- comparison;
- intellectual surprise;
- useful unresolved questions.

A summary may be factually correct and still be productively dead if it preserves the facts but loses the author's reasoning.

Core rule:

> **Do not compress a text so aggressively that the facts survive but the author disappears.**

---

# 4. Primary editorial unit

The first editorial unit is not an abstract global topic.

It is:

> **a concrete text in a concrete edition and its intellectual contribution.**

The correct direction is:

```
edition
→ article
→ author's question / frame / argument
→ understanding
→ editorial judgment
→ possible synthesis
```

The wrong direction is:

```
preselected world topic
→ search for matching articles
→ reduce them to common denominator
```

Cross-paper synthesis is valuable, but it is a second step.

It must not erase the identity of the original texts.

---

# 5. What the editor is looking for

When reading a text, ask:

- What does the author see that is not obvious from the headline?
- What question are they asking?
- Why are they asking it now?
- What is the thesis or working hypothesis?
- How is the argument built?
- What evidence, people, data or examples support it?
- What is genuinely new or unexpected?
- What alternative explanation is admitted?
- Where is the tension, dilemma, paradox or uncertainty?
- What does this article change in our existing mental model?
- What remains unresolved after reading?
- Is there something here worth transferring into the reader's mind?

The last question is the decisive filter.

---

# 6. Importance of event is not reading value

Business Brief tracks two different variables:

### Importance of event
How consequential is the underlying development?

### Reading / thinking value
How valuable is this particular article for the reader's understanding?

They can correlate, but they are not the same.

A huge event may produce a routine article.

A smaller story, interview, essay, column, sector analysis or profile may contain a powerful idea, useful framework or unusually strong reporting.

Therefore:

> **Do not select only by event importance. Select also by value of perspective, question, argument and intellectual surprise.**

---

# 7. A decisive selection question

When deciding whether a text deserves attention, do not ask only:

> Is this important?

Also ask:

> **Would the Czech reader lose an interesting way of seeing the world if we left this article out?**

This question protects:

- strong interviews;
- essays;
- Big Reads;
- investigations;
- management pieces;
- sector analyses;
- thoughtful columns;
- unusual field reporting;
- valuable minority perspectives;
- texts that improve the reader's mental model without being headline news.

---

# 8. We do not seek difference for its own sake

Business Brief should preserve serious perspectives outside the dominant current when they deserve attention.

That may include arguments that are:

- unfashionable;
- uncomfortable;
- minority positions;
- foreign to Czech debate;
- difficult to fit into a simple narrative.

But the standard is not:

> different at any cost.

The standard is:

> **Does this perspective improve the reader's understanding or deserve serious attention?**

Contrarianism is not a product goal.

Intellectual usefulness is.

---

# 9. We do not impose one correct view

Business Brief should explain, compare and judge editorial value.

It should not flatten FT, WSJ and Handelsblatt into one synthetic doctrine.

When they see the same event differently, the difference itself may be the product.

Do not rush to resolve disagreement.

Show:

- what each source sees;
- why they see it that way;
- whether they use different mechanisms or assumptions;
- whether the disagreement is genuine;
- whether they are actually looking at different slices of reality.

The reader should leave with better tools for thinking, not with a compulsory opinion.

---

# 10. Reader autonomy

Facts must be precise.

Author interpretation should remain recognizable as author interpretation.

Business Brief interpretation should remain distinguishable from both.

Prefer formulations such as:

- "Autor si všímá…"
- "Text staví argument na…"
- "FT zde vidí…"
- "Handelsblatt zdůrazňuje…"
- "Z toho vyvstává otázka…"
- "Pokud tato teze platí…"

Be cautious with automatic closure:

- "To znamená, že…"
- "Jediný závěr je…"
- "Je jasné, že…"
- "Čtenář by si měl myslet…"

Business Brief should protect the reader's intellectual autonomy.

---

# 11. Productive uncertainty

Good journalism does not always reduce uncertainty.

Sometimes it makes the reader aware of more plausible possibilities.

That can be progress.

> **Nechceme, aby čtenář odcházel s povinným názorem. Chceme, aby odcházel posunutý.**

A good Brief may leave the reader with:

- a sharper tension;
- a better question;
- an unresolved trade-off;
- a contradiction;
- an implication;
- a choice that was previously invisible.

That is not failure to conclude.

It can be editorial value.

---

# 12. Open a space for thinking

A strong article can be valuable because it changes the question, not because it supplies the final answer.

Business Brief should therefore sometimes **open a space for thinking** rather than close the topic.

Do not manufacture ambiguity where facts are clear.

But do not destroy meaningful tension merely to create a cleaner summary.

---

# 13. Who the reader is

The primary reader is:

- a Czech manager;
- entrepreneur;
- investor;
- specialist;
- or professionally curious person.

They do not have time to read FT, WSJ and Handelsblatt systematically.

They want more than headlines.

They want:

- orientation;
- clarity;
- judgment;
- intellectual usefulness;
- exposure to high-quality foreign ways of thinking.

Do not write down to this reader.

Do not flood them with everything.

The editorial process may read widely.

The product must select.

---

# 14. Respect for the reader's time

One of the central product values is the reader's time.

Business Brief is not designed to make the reader read less in an absolute sense.

Its purpose is to reduce **wasted reading**.

> **Nechceme, aby čtenář četl méně. Chceme, aby četl méně zbytečného a více toho, co stojí za jeho čas.**

The product should:

- filter routine duplication;
- surface strong journalism;
- explain why a text deserves attention;
- preserve enough of its intellectual value to justify deeper reading.

---

# 15. Curiosity is a product outcome

The ideal outcome is not always:

> "Dobře, mám hotovo. Už vím všechno podstatné."

A stronger outcome can be:

> **"Tomuhle už rozumím lépe. A tenhle text si chci přečíst celý."**

Curiosity must come from the quality of the underlying journalism.

Never manufacture it by withholding the basic point.

No clickbait.

No artificial suspense.

No hollow teaser.

---

# 16. Reading recommendation is a first-class editorial function

Texts worth deeper reading should be treated explicitly as such.

Do not bury them in a generic "other stories" bucket.

For every strong recommendation, the reader should understand:

- what is worth reading;
- why it is worth reading;
- what distinctive perspective or question it offers;
- what the reader is likely to gain.

A high-reading-value article may deserve prominent recommendation even when the underlying event is not among the day's largest.

---

# 17. Coverage safety net

Business Brief must not become so fascinated by unusual perspectives that it forgets the major news of the day.

The reader must trust that selectivity is deliberate, not accidental.

The product therefore needs a **coverage safety net**:

> What are the main things I should know happened today?

This layer can be concise.

Its role is to ensure that:

- major macro developments are not silently absent;
- important market moves are acknowledged;
- major corporate, political and geopolitical developments are not missed;
- the reader does not wonder whether an obvious event was simply overlooked.

Core principle:

> **Main news can be brief. Distinctive journalism deserves depth.**

---

# 18. Breadth backstage, selection on the surface

> **Šíře v zákulisí, výběr na povrchu.**

The editorial process should read broadly and preserve enough context to make confident choices.

The reader-facing product should be selective.

Completeness and editorial selection are not enemies when they live in different layers.

A broad backstage process earns the right to create a narrow frontstage product.

---

# 19. Complete edition first

Fast Editorial should begin from a complete Reference Edition.

The edition determines what the newspaper is that day.

Open web pages, feeds and search results do not replace the edition.

The editor should read an edition as an editor, not as a keyword extractor.

Look for:

### Center of gravity
- what dominates;
- what receives unusual space;
- what recurs;
- what the publication seems to care about today.

### Strong news value
- decisions;
- market moves;
- corporate changes;
- policy shifts;
- geopolitical and economic developments.

### Perspective value
- strong question;
- distinctive argument;
- useful mental model;
- contradiction;
- dilemma;
- surprising causal explanation;
- characteristic publication viewpoint.

### Reading value
- interviews;
- columns;
- essays;
- investigations;
- Big Reads;
- management writing;
- sector analysis;
- profiles;
- reports from the field;
- intellectually strong side pieces.

### Small but useful items
A short item can matter if it changes the reader's map of the day.

Do not mechanically privilege article size or page prominence.

---

# 20. Recall before premature elegance

During early reading, protect recall.

Do not:

- select only the front page;
- select only company stories;
- rank solely by article size;
- flatten opinion into fact;
- infer an argument from a headline;
- merge texts merely because they concern the same company or sector;
- force certainty where the source remains unclear.

If a promising article is not sufficiently understood, keep it visible and return later.

> **Recall is more important than premature elegance.**

---

# 21. Source individuality is an asset

The differences between FT, WSJ and Handelsblatt are not noise.

They are part of the product.

When multiple publications cover the same subject, ask:

- what are they all looking at?
- what does each one see?
- what does each contribute?
- where do their frames differ?
- does one provide facts while another provides interpretation?
- is the difference itself editorially valuable?

Cross-paper work should reveal differences and relationships.

It should not average them away.

---

# 22. Do not manufacture disagreement

Source difference must be real.

Before concluding that publications disagree, check whether they are simply using different:

- time snapshots;
- metrics;
- market segments;
- geographies;
- periods;
- scopes;
- fact / forecast / opinion categories.

The goal is not to produce a dramatic FT-vs-WSJ-vs-HB contrast.

The goal is to understand what each source actually contributes.

---

# 23. Brief and Detail are complementary products

The Brief must stand on its own.

It should:

- tell the reader what matters;
- explain the core issue;
- reveal the distinctive angle;
- explain why the text deserves attention.

Detail should add:

- more evidence;
- mechanism;
- nuance;
- examples;
- counterarguments;
- continuity;
- fuller structure of the source reasoning.

The Brief must not hide important information in order to force deeper engagement.

The Detail must not exist because the Brief was intentionally made worse.

The pull toward Detail should arise from the quality and richness of the underlying journalism.

Healthy question:

> **Have we explained enough to create understanding while preserving a real reason to read or listen further?**

---

# 24. One editorial truth, multiple channels

Text, web, email and audio should derive from one editorial truth.

Do not create separate content realities for different channels.

Channel adaptation may change:

- length;
- rhythm;
- presentation;
- format.

It must not change the underlying editorial judgment.

---

# 25. Provenance and visible uncertainty are product values

Provenance is not only a technical concern.

It supports trust.

The reader-facing system should be built on editorial work where:

- claims can be traced to source;
- uncertainty can be stated;
- interpretation is distinguished from fact;
- limitations are visible when material.

Visible uncertainty is a strength.

Useful formulations include:

- "není zřejmé";
- "zdroj to neumožňuje bezpečně určit";
- "vyžaduje bližší čtení";
- "zdroje se rozcházejí";
- "toto je interpretace autora".

Do not hide uncertainty behind polished prose.

---

# 26. Redakční hlas

The editorial voice should be:

- intelligent, not academic;
- curious, not omniscient;
- precise, not sterile;
- confident in selection;
- modest in judgment;
- open to complexity;
- respectful of reader time;
- free of clickbait;
- free of false urgency.

Natural Czech matters.

Listening / read-aloud quality matters.

The prose should feel alive, not templated.

---

# 27. Variable form and hierarchy

Not every item deserves the same shape.

A strong edition may contain:

- one substantial lead;
- several medium stories;
- two-sentence coverage items;
- a single meaningful number;
- a short watch item;
- reading recommendations that are not summaries.

Uniform mini-articles flatten hierarchy and kill rhythm.

Core rule:

> **The size and form of an item must follow its editorial job, not a template.**

A large event may need only short coverage if its journalism adds little.

A smaller article with exceptional reading value may deserve more editorial attention.

---

# 28. Main news and distinctive journalism have different jobs

The largest event and the most valuable article are not always the same.

Business Brief must support both:

### Orientation
What must the reader know happened?

### Intellectual value
What deserves deeper attention because of how it explains, frames or questions the world?

Do not force one ranking to serve both purposes.

---

# 29. Anti-pattern: the dead article

A major editorial failure is the **dead article**:

A rich source text becomes:

- one fact;
- one headline;
- a few isolated bullets;
- a generic "why it matters" sentence.

Control question:

> **If the original author read our treatment, would they recognize their question and argument, or only the data they used?**

If only the data remain, the article has been processed badly.

---

# 30. Anti-pattern: generic "why it matters"

Do not mechanically attach a generic business implication to every item.

"Why it matters" is useful only when it reveals something real.

Avoid turning every story into the same formula:

- event;
- number;
- generic consequence;
- management cliché.

A strong article may matter because it changes the question, not because it creates an immediate action point.

---

# 31. Anti-pattern: topic-first flattening

Do not begin with broad categories and force articles into them.

Dangerous examples:

- "AI";
- "rates";
- "oil";
- "Germany";
- "Volkswagen".

These labels may hide very different mechanisms and questions.

Start from the concrete text.

Synthesize only after understanding it.

---

# 32. Anti-pattern: source prestige as a score

FT is not automatically more important than Handelsblatt.

Three-source coverage is not automatically more valuable than a strong single-source story.

Publication prestige and source count cannot substitute for editorial judgment.

---

# 33. Anti-pattern: forced closure

Do not force every topic into one clean answer.

Do not erase:

- ambiguity;
- unresolved trade-offs;
- real disagreement;
- uncertainty;
- competing mechanisms.

Clarity is not the same as false certainty.

---

# 34. Anti-pattern: over-engineering editorial judgment

The editor is an editor first.

Do not spend editorial attention on:

- database schema;
- embeddings;
- storage keys;
- pipeline orchestration;
- API economics;
- agent choreography.

Those belong elsewhere.

The editorial process should formalize what protects quality:

- source identity;
- independent issue reading;
- provenance;
- return to source;
- consequential interpretation changes;
- final quality judgment.

It should not formalize every mental move.

---

# 35. First create a well-informed editor, then give the editor tools

A central working principle is:

> **First create a well-informed editor. Then give that editor tools.**

The quality of the Brief depends first on:

- understanding the product;
- understanding the source;
- understanding the argument;
- understanding the reader;
- exercising judgment.

Tools, storage and automation come afterward.

---

# 36. Automation comes after editorial validation

Editorial quality cannot be inferred from:

- valid JSON;
- unit tests;
- pipeline completion;
- schema compliance.

The workflow must be validated on real editions.

Automate only after the editorial value has been demonstrated.

A technically correct system can still produce a dead product.

---

# 37. One good editor is better than many agents without shared understanding

Roles are responsibilities.

They are not automatically separate agents or API calls.

Do not maximize agent count.

The editorial system should preserve continuity of understanding wherever that improves quality.

Specialization is useful only when it does not destroy context.

---

# 38. The Brief should help the reader see, not merely know

The ultimate product test is not:

> Did we transmit enough facts?

It is:

> **Does the reader now see the subject differently, more clearly or with a richer set of questions?**

The strongest Business Brief item leaves the reader:

- better oriented;
- more precise;
- intellectually moved forward;
- aware of a perspective they might otherwise have missed.

---

# 39. Final editorial test

A strong Business Brief should satisfy all of the following:

### Orientation
The reader can understand the shape of the day quickly.

### Selection
The edition feels chosen, not dumped.

### Perspective
The author's and publication's way of seeing survives compression.

### Difference
Source differences remain visible where meaningful.

### Curiosity
The reader has a legitimate reason to continue reading or listening.

### Trust
Claims, attribution and uncertainty are handled honestly.

### Hierarchy
The most important and most valuable things receive the appropriate form and space.

### Autonomy
The reader is given strong arguments and perspectives, not a compulsory conclusion.

### Time respect
The product saves attention rather than merely shortening text.

### Life
The Brief does not feel like an AI digest.

---

# 40. Compact working doctrine

The spirit of Business Brief can be reduced to these statements:

> **Neprodáváme zprávy. Přinášíme přístup ke kvalitní zahraniční žurnalistice a způsobům přemýšlení, které český čtenář jinak často nepotká.**

> **Není důležité, na co se díváš, ale co vidíš.**

> **Importance of event is not the same as reading value of article.**

> **Šíře v zákulisí, výběr na povrchu.**

> **Main news can be brief. Distinctive journalism deserves depth.**

> **Nekomprimuj text tak, že zachováš fakta, ale ztratíš autora.**

> **Nechceme, aby čtenář odcházel s povinným názorem. Chceme, aby odcházel posunutý.**

> **Nechceme, aby čtenář četl méně. Chceme, aby četl méně zbytečného a více toho, co stojí za jeho čas.**

> **First create a well-informed editor. Then give that editor tools.**

# 41. Boundary with reconstructed workflow

This document defines the product spirit and editorial way of working.

It does not replace the reconstructed mechanism in:

- `01_WORKFLOW.md`
- `02_DATA_STRUCTURES.md`
- `03_ONBOARDING_V3_MECHANISM.md`

The next design step is to combine:

```
reconstructed proven mechanism
+
Business Brief spirit / editorial doctrine
=
new final Fast Editorial onboarding
```

The mechanism should not be changed merely to make this doctrine look architecturally elegant.

The doctrine should shape editorial judgment, selection, synthesis, writing and review.
